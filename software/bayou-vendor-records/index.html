Bayou Vendor Records

A vendor-record system whose most important design decision is a refusal: it records that a document arrived and when it lapses, and it will not tell you whether the document is any good.

Prototype
Records · Workflow systems
2026

How do you build a compliance-adjacent tool that is genuinely useful without pretending to be the compliance decision?

01What it is

Bayou Vendor Records is a lightweight system of record for residential property managers in South Louisiana. It tracks, per vendor, whether a small fixed set of documents has arrived, what dates the client has given for them, and what needs a human this week. Once a week it produces a Weekly Rundown: everything sorted into four lanes, readable in one pass.

It is not a vendor-management platform, and describing it as one would miss the interesting part. The interesting part is where the system stops.

02The problem

A property manager is accountable for a long tail of vendor relationships across a portfolio, and for keeping records on each one current enough to answer a question when a work order, an insurance query or an audit arrives. In practice the receipt status of a W-9, a certificate of insurance or a contractor licence lives in some mix of inboxes, shared drives, paper folders and whoever touched the file last.

That arrangement works right up until someone needs to find something quickly and nobody can say with confidence which record is current. The failure is not that the documents are missing — it is that their state is unknown, and reconstructing it costs more attention than the question was worth.

03The system

The atomic unit is not the document. It is the receipt status of a document: a fact about whether something arrived, and when, recorded from what the client supplied. That choice determines everything downstream, including what the system is allowed to say.

  1. 01RequestedA vendor is added from the manager’s own portfolio and a small fixed set of documents is expected of them.
  2. 02RecordedReceipt status is recorded per vendor, per document, with a date. Only that it arrived — never its contents and never its sufficiency.
  3. 03DatedClient-supplied expiration or review dates are attached, so a record has a future as well as a present.
  4. 04Sorted weeklyEvery item falls into exactly one of four lanes. The lane, not the record, is what the manager reads.
  5. 05Draft heldAny follow-up to a vendor is prepared as a draft with its context attached, and held. Nothing leaves the office unsigned.
  6. 06Human actsThe manager approves, sends, or decides it is not worth chasing. The system records what happened; it does not decide.

Fig. 1 — the record lifecycle. Every arc ends at a person.

04The four lanes

The Weekly Rundown is the product’s real interface. It exists because a list of every vendor is not actionable and a notification per event is noise; a weekly sort into four buckets is the smallest thing that turns a pile of records into a short queue of decisions.

Needs attention

Items where client approval is the next required step before any outbound contact happens.

Approaching review date

Documents whose client-supplied expiration or review date falls inside the next sixty days.

Awaiting response

The outreach log — drafts prepared, not sent until the manager signs off, and then tracked.

Recorded as received

Items whose receipt status was recorded as received during the current week. Confirmation, not a to-do.

Fig. 2 — the attention model. Four lanes, one weekly read.

The tracked record set is deliberately short — seven lines, readable at a glance: W-9 receipt status, certificate-of-insurance receipt status, client-supplied policy dates, contractor or business-licence reference status, follow-up draft prepared, the attention queue, and the weekly summary itself. A manager can read the whole list and know exactly what the system will and will not say about a vendor.

05Boundaries, as a design decision

Vendor paperwork sits next to real legal and insurance consequences. A tool in that space can drift very easily into implying it has checked something it has not, and the cost of that drift lands on the customer, not the vendor. So the boundary is stated as product behaviour, in the same type size as the features:

  • Does not determine legal compliance. Sufficiency of a record is a judgement the manager makes with their own advisors.
  • Does not determine insurance adequacy. It records that a certificate arrived and the date given for it — not whether the coverage is right.
  • Does not approve vendors. There is no approved state in the system, because approval is not a thing the software is competent to grant.
  • Does not verify contractor qualifications. A licence reference being filed is not a licence being checked, and the field is named accordingly.
  • Does not send vendor communication without client approval. Every outbound draft stops at a person.
  • Does not store W-9 forms or taxpayer identification numbers. The storage boundary and the judgement boundary are the same decision: hold the status, not the artefact.

Two things make this structural rather than decorative. First, the boundary statement is an acknowledgement the prospective client has to tick before the inquiry form will submit — it is a gate on the way in, not a disclaimer on the way out. Second, the field names carry it: every tracked value is worded as receipt status recorded from client-provided information, so the schema itself cannot be read as a verification.

The last one is the part I would defend hardest. If the field were called coi_valid, no amount of footer text would stop someone treating it as a compliance check a year from now. Naming is a boundary that survives the next person to touch the system.

06My role

Product conception, the record-state model and its vocabulary, the weekly attention model and its four lanes, the scope boundary and where it is enforced, the interface and copy, the regional positioning, the commercial offer, and deployment of the live pilot.

The application was built and directed on an agentic development platform — Polsia — rather than from a blank repository. The framework scaffolding is the platform’s; the system this is a system of — what a record is, what states it can hold, what the software is allowed to assert, and what has to stop at a person — is mine.

07What exists now

A deployed application with a public product surface, account creation and sign-in, the workflow and record set specified in detail, a worked illustration of the Weekly Rundown and the status board, a scope-acknowledgement gate on the inquiry path, and a defined commercial offer: a Founding Five cohort of South Louisiana property-management businesses, a stated setup and first-month price, up to twenty-five active vendors per client, and no automatic renewal.

08Current status and limitations

Pilot stage. Every figure on the live site is illustrative, and says so.

The dashboard preview, the vendor table and all four Weekly Rundown lanes are populated with composed sample data, labelled as fictional on the page. That is honest of the product, and it is also the reason this is filed as a prototype and not a showcase: the founding cohort is open, not filled, and no operating portfolio has been run through it in public.

No adoption, retention, time-saved or volume figure appears anywhere on this page, because none has been measured.

Also:

  • The working application is behind account creation, so the publicly inspectable surface is the product specification and the illustrated board rather than live tooling.
  • The repository is private. The implementation stack is not enumerated here — see the technology note in the sidebar.
  • Scope is capped by design at twenty-five active vendors per client and to one region. Whether the weekly-lane model still reads well at ten times that size is untested.

09How this differs from TerritoryOne

Both are prototypes in this wing and both are small operational systems, but they are built around different questions. Bayou is about state over time: what do we hold, when does it lapse, and what needs a person this week. TerritoryOne is about allocation at a moment: is this request real, and who owns it. One accumulates and expires; the other validates and assigns.
