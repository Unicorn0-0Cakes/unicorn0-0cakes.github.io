What does a small contractor actually buy when they buy “help with Google”?

01What it is

Lantern Reply is a Google Business Profile service for independent HVAC companies in South Louisiana. It has two surfaces. The first is the Map Check: a free, manually written read of a company’s public profile that comes back as three prioritised actions in an email, with no call attached. The second is a monthly engagement — the Founding Client Offer — that carries out that kind of work on a fixed cadence for one business location.

It is a service, not a piece of software, but it was designed the way software is designed: a defined input, a defined state it moves through, a defined output, and an explicit list of things it will not do.

02The problem

An owner-operated HVAC company runs trucks across several parishes and is listed at one address. Reviews arrive in a burst during a hot stretch or after a storm and get answered three weeks later, or not at all. The last photo on the profile is from the previous winter. None of that is a marketing failure exactly — it is what happens when the person who would fix it is the same person who is on a roof.

The commercial problem underneath is worse: the category that sells into it is full of vendors promising rankings, and a buyer has no way of telling a real service from a confident one. So the product’s first job is not visibility. It is legibility — making it obvious, before any money changes hands, exactly what is going to be done and what is not.

03The system

The free tier and the paid tier are the same pipeline run at different lengths. The Map Check stops after step three; the engagement continues in a monthly loop through steps four to six.

  1. 01IntakeFour fields: business name, business email, trade, primary city or parish. Nothing else is asked for up front.
  2. 02Manual reviewA person reads the public profile across five fixed dimensions — accuracy, categories and services, service-area clarity, review activity, photos and posts.
  3. 03Three prioritiesThe finding is compressed to the three highest-priority actions and sent by email. The count is the product, not a summary of a longer report.
  4. 04Monthly draftsReview responses, profile posts and field edits are prepared against a fixed monthly allowance rather than an open-ended scope.
  5. 05Owner approvalNothing is published until the owner has read it and agreed to the wording. This is the gate the whole service is built around.
  6. 06Publish & reportApproved work goes live; the month closes with a plain-language report of what changed and where the next month lands.

Fig. 1 — the service pipeline. Steps 01–03 are the free Map Check; 04–06 repeat monthly.

04What the customer actually buys

The engagement is deliberately countable. It covers one business location, at a published rate — a one-time setup fee and a monthly fee, month to month, no annual term — and the monthly deliverable is a list with numbers in it rather than a promise: an opening profile audit, a branded review-request link and QR code, two request templates, up to twenty individualised review-response drafts, four profile posts, field and service-area edits, and a monthly report.

Every one of those is a thing that either happened or did not. That was the point. A buyer in this category cannot verify a ranking claim, but anyone can count four posts.

05Constraint design

The most deliberate part of the product is the list of things it refuses, published on the marketing page rather than buried in the terms:

  • No fake offices
  • No purchased reviews
  • No review gating — nobody is filtered out of being asked
  • No invented service areas
  • No guaranteed rankings, ratings, calls, leads or revenue

Three of those five are refusals of tactics that would work in the short term, which is why they belong on the front page: the constraint is only meaningful where it costs something. The sixth rule — client approval before publication — is the one that shapes the workflow, because it forces every deliverable into a draft state with an owner who has to sign it off, and rules out the whole class of “we’ll handle it” automation the category normally sells.

The same logic runs through the sample deliverable. Rather than showing a real client’s results, the site publishes worked examples for fictional businesses, each labelled as fictional on the page it appears on. A prospective buyer gets to see the format of the work product without the format being propped up by a case study that would have to be taken on trust.

06My role

I defined and built the product: the concept, the two-tier service architecture, the intake and approval workflow, the five review dimensions, the contents and limits of the monthly deliverable, the refusal list, the positioning and voice, the pricing structure, the interface, and the launch of the live site on its own domain.

The application itself was built and directed on an agentic development platform — Polsia — rather than assembled from a blank repository. What I brought to it is the part that is specific to this system: what the product is, who it is for, what moves through it, what it is allowed to claim, and what it must not do. The framework scaffolding underneath is the platform’s.

07What exists now

A live service on its own domain, with a public marketing surface, a working intake form for the free Map Check, a published sample-deliverable page, published pricing, a service terms page, a privacy notice, a blog and an authenticated client sign-in. The commercial offer is defined and open: a founding cohort capped at five South Louisiana businesses, at a rate stated to close when those slots are filled.

08Limitations

There are no outcome numbers here, and there will not be

The product tells its own customers it does not guarantee rankings, ratings, calls, leads or revenue. A portfolio case study that then reported a ranking improvement or a revenue figure would be contradicting the thing it is describing. What is demonstrated here is a designed and deployed service system — not a measured commercial result.

The founding cohort is open rather than filled, and no client outcomes are claimed, because none have been measured.

Also worth stating plainly:

  • The client-side application is behind sign-in, so what is publicly inspectable is the marketing surface, the intake path and the sample deliverable — not the working client tooling.
  • The repository is private. The implementation stack is therefore not enumerated on this page; see the technology note in the sidebar.
  • The service is scoped to one location per engagement and to one trade in one region. That is a deliberate limit, but it is a limit.

09Why it sits in Design

Because the artefact is not the website. It is the service: what gets asked, in what order, by whom, with what held back for approval, and with which claims ruled out before anyone is tempted to make them. The visual and verbal system exists to make that structure legible to someone deciding whether to trust it — which is the same problem as an identity, solved with a workflow instead of a mark.
