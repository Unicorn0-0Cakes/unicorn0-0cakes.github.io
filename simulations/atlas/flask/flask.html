Evolution
in a Flask

Twelve flasks. The same strain in all of them, the same thin sugar medium, the same incubator. Every morning one part in a hundred is moved into fresh medium, and every five hundred generations a sample is frozen. Nothing else happens. Nothing else needs to.

You run the bench. You decide the conditions, and then you decide what to look at — because looking costs time, and there is never enough of it. Fitness here is not a number the simulation hands you. It is a competition you have to set up, against something you were careful enough to freeze.

On the history. The long-term evolution experiment began on 24 February 1988 with twelve populations founded from a single strain of Escherichia coli, propagated by daily hundredfold transfer in a minimal medium containing 25 micrograms of glucose per millilitre. Samples have been frozen every 500 generations throughout, which is why evolved bacteria can be thawed and made to compete directly against their own ancestors. Published findings include a fitness gain that decelerates without ever quite stopping, repeated mutation of the same handful of genes across populations that have never met, hypermutability evolving in about half of them, and — in one population, after roughly thirty thousand generations — the appearance of aerobic citrate use, an ability the ancestor did not have and the other eleven never found.

This simulation is historically informed. It is not a reconstruction. Every screen labels which of its numbers are documented, which are estimated from general microbiology, and which were invented so that the thing would run.
