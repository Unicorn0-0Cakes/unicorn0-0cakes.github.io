Inside
the Atom

A foil of gold two thousand atoms thick, a pencil of alpha particles, and a zinc-sulphide screen you can swing to any angle around it. Almost everything goes straight through. The question is what the handful that do not are telling you.

You operate the beam and the detector. Nothing in the apparatus shows you an atom; you get counts at angles you chose, with the statistics those counts happen to have, and you have to decide from them whether the positive charge in the foil is spread through each atom or concentrated at its centre.

On the history. In 1909 Geiger and Marsden reported that about one alpha particle in eight thousand falling on a thick platinum plate came back out on the side it went in. In 1911 Rutherford published the single-scattering theory that accounts for it, and in 1913 Geiger and Marsden tested that theory from 5° to 150°, counting more than a hundred thousand scintillations. This instrument implements the two competing angular distributions as Rutherford set them out side by side in the 1911 paper. It is not a reconstruction of the apparatus: the geometry is schematic, the exposures are counted in particles rather than in weeks, and the numbers that had no counterpart in 1913 are labelled where they appear.
