The Falling
Charge

A droplet of oil, a millimetre of still air, and two brass plates. Nothing in the apparatus shows you a charge. What you can see is how fast a droplet falls, and how its fall changes when you switch on a field — and that is all Millikan could see either.

The elementary charge is not visible anywhere in this instrument. It becomes visible only as a spacing that keeps recurring in careful measurements. You will calibrate, track droplets through a reticle, decide which observations you are willing to defend, write down why you threw the others away, and then commit to an answer before you are allowed to see the accepted value.

The preferred scientific mode. The accepted value is sealed until you lock your analysis.

The same seed generates the same droplets with the same hidden charges and the same instrument errors. Your own actions decide which of them you measure.

Read the methods
What this is, and what it is not. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the elementary charge is exact by definition: e = 1.602 176 634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, and the ampere is defined in terms of it. No experiment measures it any more. What this instrument recreates is the inference — the reasoning by which physicists first established that charge comes in a common unit at all, and estimated its size from droplet motion. The exact value is used internally to build the droplets, and it stays sealed until you lock your analysis.

Status: research prototype. No physicist has reviewed the equations, signs, correction factors or explanatory language. Model comparison is not implemented, each droplet is measured through a single transit where Millikan used many, and 24 of the 40 registered parameters are marked not yet calibrated. The limitations are written down rather than glossed. Simulated data are evidence about this model, not about the world.
