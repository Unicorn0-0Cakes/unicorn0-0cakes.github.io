01 The source

The research question

Can a noisy magnetic trace collected above the ocean floor reveal the hidden motion, polarity history and age of oceanic crust? The instrument puts that question in its inverse form: the operator observes effects — one scalar field measurement every kilometre or so, from a sensor towed two or three kilometres above rock it cannot see — and has to reconstruct the system that produced them.

Historical background

Through the 1950s, magnetic surveys at sea returned patterns of anomaly over the ocean floor that had no counterpart on land. In 1963 Frederick Vine and Drummond Matthews published a short letter in Nature proposing that those anomalies are a record of a reversing geomagnetic field, written into crust as it forms at a ridge and then carried away from it, so that the pattern is repeated on both flanks [1].

Lawrence Morley, of the Geological Survey of Canada, reached the same interpretation independently. His letter setting it out was rejected; the argument appeared in 1964 in a Royal Society of Canada volume with A. Larochelle [2]. The hypothesis is properly called Vine–Matthews–Morley, and this page uses that name. The USGS account of the episode also notes that field reversals had already been demonstrated in continental rocks before the marine interpretation was proposed, and that the potassium–argon reversal chronology assembled by Cox, Doell and Dalrymple was what allowed the marine pattern to be dated [4].

In 1968 Heirtzler, Dickson, Herron, Pitman and Le Pichon took the argument the other way round: assuming the interpretation, they used the anomaly pattern itself to build a polarity timescale reaching back about 80 million years [3].

This instrument is not a reconstruction of any of that work. No survey in it corresponds to a real cruise, no preset is named after a real ridge, every world it builds is generic and synthetic, and every noise figure is invented. What it reproduces is the shape of the inference — measure, compare, commit, then check — not the data.

02 Rules and equations

What the magnetometer observes, and what stays hidden

The instrument returns one quantity: the total-field magnetic anomaly in nanotesla, at a recorded position along the trackline, once per sample interval. That is all. Everything else — the ridge axis, the two half-spreading rates, whether the flanks match, the polarity sequence in the rock, the crustal age, the generating model itself — is hidden until the operator commits an interpretation, and is hidden in the exported data file as well.

The conversion everything hangs off is a unit identity, not a physical result:

distance (km) = 10 × half rate (cm/yr) × age (Ma)

because 1 cm/yr is 10 km per million years. A polarity interval of duration D Ma therefore occupies a band 

interval width (km) = 10 × half rate (cm/yr) × D (Ma)

on one side of the axis. Across the ridge the same interval spans that width on each flank — twice as much in total when the flanks are equal. A half rate and a full rate are never interchanged anywhere in the code; the variables are named halfRateLeftCmYr, halfRateRightCmYr and fullRateCmYr so that the mistake would be visible. Age at the active axis is exactly zero and increases away from it.

Survey geometry

With α the angle between the trackline and the ridge axis, the ridge-normal displacement after an along-track distance s is 

x = s · sin α

so a perpendicular crossing is α = 90°, and an oblique track stretches every apparent band width along the track by 1/sin α. A rate read straight off an oblique trace without correcting for that is too high by exactly that factor. Below 30° the instrument warns; below 15° it refuses to run the line, because the ridge-normal coverage has collapsed and the survey carries almost no information about the rate. Zero-information geometry is never silently allowed.

Polarity chronology

Two sources, always distinguished on screen and in the export.

Published. Normal-polarity intervals for the last 5.23 Ma, transcribed from a single published table [8]. Every boundary carries the attribution the table gives it, kept in the src field of each record in data/polarity-timescale.js; no age in that file is anonymous. The field is normal at age zero — the Brunhes — as the source has it. Crust older than 5.23 Ma is left unmagnetised rather than extrapolated, because the instrument has no source for it.

Synthetic. A seeded sequence of irregular intervals, labelled synthetic wherever it appears. No boundary in it corresponds to a real reversal, and the two are never mixed without saying which is which.

The published table is a compilation from the mid-1990s. Later work — Ogg (2020) in Geologic Time Scale 2020 being the obvious case — revises several boundaries by tens of thousands of years; the Matuyama–Brunhes boundary is quoted there at 0.773 Ma against 0.780 Ma here. Those differences are far below what a single noisy transect in this instrument can resolve, but they are real, and the table carries no uncertainties of its own.

The forward magnetic model

This is the part that has to be right, because it is what separates a magnetic profile from a coloured stripe diagram. The magnetised layer is treated as a set of two-dimensional rectangular prisms, infinite along strike, uniformly magnetised, sharing one top and one bottom depth. That is the classical marine-magnetics forward problem of Talwani and Heirtzler [5], re-derived from first principles — with the errors in the 1964 derivation identified and corrected — by Kravchinsky and colleagues [6]. Their equations (4)–(6) are the ones implemented.

Let x and z be the position of a source element relative to the sensor, z positive downward, and Jx, Jz the components of magnetisation in A/m. For a body infinite in the strike direction the potential of an element is 

dΩ = (Jxx + Jzz) / [2π(x² + z²)] dx dz

and the vertical and horizontal field components follow by differentiation:

dV = [2Jxxz − Jz(x² − z²)] / [2π(x² + z²)²] dx dz
dH = [2Jzxz + Jx(x² − z²)] / [2π(x² + z²)²] dx dz

Integrating over a rectangle x ∈ [x1, x2], z ∈ [z1, z2] is elementary, since 

∬ (x² − z²)/(x² + z²)² dx dz = arctan(x/z)
∬      xz /(x² + z²)² dx dz = −½ ln(x² + z²)

Writing A = arctan(x/z), R = ½ ln(x² + z²) and D[·] for the double difference F(x2,z2) − F(x1,z2) − F(x2,z1) + F(x1,z1): 

V = (1/2π) · D[ −JxR − JzA ]
H = (1/2π) · D[ −JzR + JxA ]

The sensor is always above the layer, so z ≥ z1 > 0 throughout and arctan(x/z) never crosses a branch cut. There are no special cases and no singularities inside the domain of use.

Total field, and the effective inclination

A marine magnetometer measures the scalar total field. For an anomaly small against the main field the standard reduction is to project the anomalous vector onto the main-field direction [6, eq. 13]. This instrument works in the reduced two-dimensional geometry conventional in marine magnetics, in which the strike azimuth and the declination are folded into a single effective inclination Ieff shared by the magnetisation and the field [7]. Setting Jx = J cos Ieff, Jz = J sin Ieff and projecting on the same direction collapses the whole thing to one line, per unit magnetisation: 

T = −sin(2 Ieff) · D[R] + cos(2 Ieff) · D[A]

At Ieff = 90° — the magnetic-pole case — this reduces to T = −D[A], the anomaly is symmetric about each block, and a symmetric world produces a symmetric profile. Below 90° the anomalies skew, which is real and is why the control exists. Converting a latitude, a declination and a strike azimuth into Ieff is not implemented: the operator sets it directly, and anomalous skewness is not modelled at all.

With magnetisation in A/m and lengths in km, the constant that turns the expression above into nanotesla is μ₀ × 10⁹ / 2π = 200; the length unit cancels because the two-dimensional expression is scale-free in x and z.

Why the trace is smooth when the crust is not. The blocks have sharp edges; the field they produce does not. Raising the sensor reduces the amplitude and suppresses the high-frequency detail — upward continuation is a low-pass filter whose corner is set by the sensor's height above the source. Neighbouring blocks of opposite polarity partly cancel. The result is a continuous trace whose peaks do not sit over block centres and whose zero crossings do not land on the polarity boundaries. Both of those are asserted as tests, not as claims.

The noise model

Every stochastic effect comes from one seeded generator (mulberry32, Box–Muller for Gaussians), drawn in a fixed order — navigation, then noise, then dropout, sample by sample — so that the same seed and the same settings return byte-identical arrays.

  • Instrument noise. Gaussian, independent between samples, 1σ set in nT.
  • Regional trend. A constant offset plus a linear ramp in nT per 100 km plus a small curvature, all along-track. It cannot be separated from the crustal signal except by fitting it, which every candidate model is allowed to do.
  • Navigation uncertainty. The reading is taken where the ship actually was and logged where the ship thought it was.
  • Dropout. Lost readings are marked missing. They are dropped from every fit and are blank in the export. They are never set to zero and never interpolated across.
  • Finite survey. Length, spacing, altitude, speed and obliquity are all the operator's, and all cost or constrain something.

Inference and model comparison

Each candidate model has a structural part, which builds a crust and computes the profile the forward model says it would produce, and a set of linear nuisance parameters — an amplitude, and a per-line offset, ramp and curvature — fitted by ordinary least squares. Those nuisance terms are fitted for every candidate. Fitting them for one and not the others would decide the comparison in advance.

The automatic search does not use one grid. A chronology with two dozen boundaries acts as a long ruler: a rate error of one part in fifty displaces the outermost boundary of a 100 km profile by four kilometres, more than the width of the short subchrons out there, and the residual jumps from the noise floor to the full variance of the data. The misfit surface is a needle. So the search works outward from the axis, in the order a person works: find the axial anomaly by sweeping each flank's rate against a window holding about 1.7 Ma of crust; keep the best few positions; open the window to about seventy per cent of the profile and refine axis and both rates together, because they are strongly coupled; open it to the whole profile and refine again; then rescore every survivor on the same data. Candidates within a stage are always scored on the same samples, or on a residual ratio normalised against a nuisance-only fit on those samples when the window is candidate-dependent.

The four candidates are compared on:

  • root-mean-square residual, in nT, against the operator's own stated instrument noise;
  • correlation between observed and predicted;
  • lag-1 autocorrelation of the residual — the statistic that says a model is wrong in a structured way rather than merely imprecise;
  • parameter count, and ΔAICc, which charges each model for its parameters;
  • performance on a held-out transect, when more than one line has been run: the models are fitted on the earlier lines and scored on the last, with only the regional nuisance terms refitted there, so what is being tested is whether the structure transfers.

When the difference in held-out error between the two leading models is inside the sampling noise on that difference, the instrument reports "unable to distinguish" and says another transect would help. It does not rank them anyway. ΔAICc is described on screen as a comparison aid and never as a probability that a model is true, and the word "proved" does not appear.

04 What is simplified

The forward model is a simplified pedagogical implementation of a published physical model. The potential-theory expressions are the real ones and are implemented exactly; the world they are applied to is deliberately thin. Not modelled, at all:

  • variable crustal thickness — one layer, one thickness, everywhere;
  • variable magnetisation intensity — one value for every block, with no decay of remanence with age and no chemical remanence acquired later;
  • alteration of oceanic crust;
  • sediment cover;
  • bathymetric effects on the magnetics — the seafloor relief is drawn from the depth–age relation [9] but the top of the source layer is held at a constant depth below the sensor. The section the operator sees and the section the forward model uses are not the same section, and that inconsistency is deliberate, so that sensor altitude remains a single controllable variable;
  • latitude and true field inclination, magnetic declination, and non-vertical magnetisation, except through the single effective-inclination parameter described in §02;
  • anomalous skewness;
  • transform faults, ridge jumps and propagating rifts;
  • changes in spreading rate through time — each flank has one constant rate for its whole history;
  • three-dimensional source geometry — everything is infinite along strike;
  • ship magnetic contamination and heading error;
  • diurnal field variation and external magnetic disturbance, including storms;
  • uncertainty in the reversal chronology itself — boundary ages are treated as exact;
  • real survey calibration: base-station correction, heading calibration, crossover adjustment, levelling between lines, and the removal of the main field, which here is simply assumed to have been done perfectly.

The instrument also simplifies the survey. Ship-hours are a budget, not a cost model. The animation runs a line about fifteen hundred times faster than a ship would. Sample spacing is free, when in practice it is tied to speed and logging rate.

05 What this cannot establish

  • That the real seafloor spreads, or that the real geomagnetic field reverses. A fit here shows that synthetic observations are consistent with a synthetic world of a particular kind. It is evidence about the model, not about the Earth.
  • Any spreading rate, axis position or reversal age as a measurement. Nothing in this instrument was fitted to real data.
  • That one noisy transect can settle the question. It generally cannot, and the instrument is built to say so — including by making worlds in which the honest answer is "unable to distinguish".
  • That a low residual means a correct model. A model with enough free parameters can follow almost any wiggle; that is exactly what the correlated-magnetisation candidate is there to demonstrate.
  • That a fit statistic is equivalent to truth, or that ΔAICc is a probability.
  • That correlation between an observed and a predicted profile is proof of anything.
  • That every ridge is symmetric. Real spreading is often close to symmetric and rarely exactly so.
  • That geomagnetic reversals are caused by plate motion. They are not, and nothing here implies it: the two are independent processes, and the pattern in the rock exists only because they happen at the same time.
  • That the magnetic stripes are visible from a ship. They are not. The observed profile is a continuous scalar record, not an image of the stripes, and the instrument never calls it one.
  • That the model reproduces every feature of real marine magnetic data. It reproduces very few.
  • That a working interface establishes scientific validity.

06 Validation status

Evidence classification

The catalogue badge is Uncalibrated prototype: internally consistent, with nothing fitted to data. That is the correct level. The forward model is a faithful implementation of published potential-theory expressions [5, 6] and is checked against independent properties of those expressions, but no parameter in the instrument has been calibrated against a measured marine magnetic profile, and no output has been compared with one. Raising the badge would require exactly that comparison, shown here as a table.

What is tested

Two suites run from one source, headless with node tests/model-tests.js and in the browser at tests/model-tests.html, plus a headless render-and-drive test at node tests/smoke.js. At the last run: 87 model assertions passed, 0 failed; 17 smoke assertions passed, 0 failed.

Reproducibility and random seeds

The seed is shown, editable, and written into the export. Restart same seed rebuilds the identical hidden world and the identical noise realisation; New seed draws a new world. The generator is mulberry32 with a FNV-1a stream mixer, so (seed, "noise", 2) is a reproducible distinct stream and the result is identical in every browser and in node. Every generated array is checked for non-finite values and throws rather than quietly drawing an empty chart.

Known gaps in validation

  • No comparison against a measured marine magnetic profile. This is the gap that keeps the badge where it is.
  • The automatic search is reliable on the cases in the test suite (13 of 14 synthetic worlds recovered to within 3 km and 10% at signal-to-noise above about 2), but it is a search, not a proof of identifiability. The one failure was at 60 nT noise on a ~150 nT signal, where failing is the correct outcome; the instrument reports the poor fit rather than a confident answer.
  • The browser rendering has been exercised through a headless DOM and canvas stub, not through a real browser at the time of writing.

07 Version history

0.1.0 — August 2026. First release. Two-dimensional prism forward model after Talwani & Heirtzler as re-derived by Kravchinsky et al., reduced to the effective-inclination form. Published polarity chronology for 0–5.23 Ma with per-boundary attribution, plus seeded synthetic chronologies. Four modes, eight presets, three generating models.
