01 What I do

I build systems in order to understand them. That has taken the shape of nine browser instruments that rebuild documented scientific experiments; a computer-vision pipeline that turns a photograph of a key into a printable 3D model, shipped across four delivery surfaces; machine-learning models for expression recognition, digit classification and predictive maintenance; a three-tier research framework for archaeological site detection; and, for several years before any of that, visual systems for jewellery houses and property listings.

Those look like unrelated fields. They are the same activity performed on different material. Each one is a system with a boundary, a set of levers you can pull, and a set of quantities you can only get at through instruments that are worse than the thing they measure. Learning to be precise about that third category — what you cannot see directly, and what it therefore costs to claim anything about it — is the part that transfers.

It is also why this portfolio is arranged the way it is. Every project carries a status, every status has a written definition, and unfinished work is labelled unfinished on the card rather than in a footnote nobody reads.

02 How I work

Four habits that show up in everything on this site, whatever the medium.

Make the constraint visible

The simulations charge bench hours for measurements, so you have to decide what not to look for. That is not a difficulty setting — it is the actual condition every experimenter works under, and a model that hides it teaches the wrong lesson.

Report the thing that weakens the claim

The emotion detector's class imbalance sits next to its accuracy. The archaeology framework opens by saying it has trained nothing. Where notebook outputs were lost, the page says the number is unverifiable instead of quoting one anyway.

Build the smallest thing that answers the question

The turbofan model is a ridge regression, deliberately, so that the preprocessing and the sensor-drift analysis are the visible work. The deepest SVHN network was abandoned because its training cost made the search impractical, not because it scored badly.

Design the container, not just the contents

The Simulations atlas has an evidence badge with five defined levels and a dashed status pending state, because a schema that makes overclaiming awkward is more reliable than an intention to be careful.
