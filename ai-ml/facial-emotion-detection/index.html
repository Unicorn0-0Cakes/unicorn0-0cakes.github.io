Facial Emotion Detection

A transfer-learning classifier for four facial expressions, reported at the accuracy it reached rather than the accuracy it was aiming for.

Showcase
VGG16 · TensorFlow/Keras
March 2025

Which architecture balances recognition accuracy against what it costs to train?

01What it is

A convolutional classifier that sorts facial photographs into four expressions — happy, sad, neutral and surprise — using VGG16 as a frozen feature extractor with a fine-tuned head. The work sits in affective computing: the attempt to get machines to register something about human emotional state.

02Why

Expressions are a good problem for learning what a classifier can and cannot do, because humans disagree with each other about them. Happiness is easy — a smile is a large, unambiguous change in facial geometry. Neutral is hard, because “neutral” is defined by the absence of the features the model has learned to look for. That asymmetry is visible in the results, and it is the interesting part.

03My role

Dataset exploration and class-distribution analysis, architecture selection, the training loop with per-run logging of hyperparameters and metrics, evaluation, and the write-up. Rather than sweeping many architectures, this project committed to one and iterated it, logging each run so the iterations could be compared against one another.

Figures as reported in the project notebook, 3 March 2025. These are the numbers the model produced; they have not been rounded up or re-run to a better seed.

Validation and test accuracy sit within two points of each other, which suggests the model generalises about as well as it fits — no severe overfitting, and no evidence that a longer run would have closed the gap. Precision exceeding recall by roughly three points means the model is somewhat conservative: when it commits to a class it is more often right than its overall hit rate suggests, and it misses more than it misfires.

05What the numbers do not say

The dataset is not balanced. Happy, sad and neutral each account for roughly 26% of the images; surprise is around 21%. A four-class problem with that distribution has a majority-class baseline near 26%, so 72.66% is a real result rather than an artefact — but the underrepresented class is also the one most likely to be carrying the error, and a single overall accuracy figure hides that entirely.

This is why the class distribution is reported here next to the accuracy rather than in a methods appendix. An accuracy number without its class balance is not a claim anyone can check.

06Limitations

  • Four classes only. Real expression recognition needs at least six, and the difficult ones — fear, disgust, contempt — are not among these four.
  • Per-class precision and recall were computed during the run but are not preserved in the committed notebook, so the specific confusion pattern cannot be reported here without re-running.
  • No demographic breakdown of the dataset was performed, so nothing can be said about whether performance is even across groups. For a system that classifies faces, that is a material gap, not a minor one.
  • Accuracy in the low seventies is well short of deployment quality for anything consequential.

07What I would do next

Re-run with per-class metrics and a confusion matrix preserved in the notebook, then address the surprise class specifically — either with augmentation or with class weighting — and measure whether overall accuracy moves or simply redistributes. The second outcome would be more informative than the first.
