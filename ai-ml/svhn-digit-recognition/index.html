SVHN Digit Recognition

Two dense networks and two convolutional ones on the same problem, compared on accuracy and on the thing accuracy tables usually leave out: what it took to get there.

Showcase
TensorFlow/Keras · CNN
February 2025

Where does added depth stop paying for itself?

01What it is

Digit classification on Street View House Numbers — photographs of house numbers cropped from Google Street View, which is a harder problem than it sounds because the crops contain neighbouring digits, motion blur, odd lighting and every typeface a sign painter ever chose. Four architectures were built and compared on the same data: two fully-connected networks and two convolutional ones.

02Why

The stated goal was accuracy. The actual finding was about cost. It is easy to write a comparison table in which the deepest model wins, and much more useful to record which models you could actually train, tune and iterate on with the hardware you had.

03My role

Dataset preparation and the train / validation / test split, all four architectures, the training runs, the comparison, and the write-up.

04The four models

ANN 1
A baseline dense network. Adam at 0.001, categorical cross-entropy. Adequate on simple classification, clearly out of its depth on images — no spatial structure to exploit, so every pixel is an independent feature.
ANN 2
Deeper, with dropout and batch normalisation added. Training stabilised and overfitting reduced, but the added parameters bought longer training rather than better generalisation. Neither dense model was the right tool.
CNN 3
Convolutional layers with max pooling and Leaky ReLU. Immediate improvement — spatial features are exactly what this problem has. Validation accuracy tracked training accuracy closely, indicating minimal overfitting. The model I would keep.
CNN 4
Deeper and wider. Training required repeated restarts because of memory limits, which made hyperparameter search impractical rather than merely slow. Abandoned — not for scoring badly, but for being untunable on the hardware available.

05Outcome

On the numbers

The notebook's own analysis reports the best convolutional model at roughly 97% accuracy. That figure is quoted from the write-up, not re-verified: the notebook was committed with its cell outputs cleared, so no stored evaluation output survives to check it against. It is recorded here as an author-reported figure and should be read as one.

The comparison itself does not depend on that number. The ordering was unambiguous — convolutional beat dense by a wide margin, and the deeper CNN was rejected on cost rather than on score. CNN 3 gave the best ratio of accuracy to training time, and the improvements it still wanted were cheap ones: batch normalisation for stability, dropout against overfitting, augmentation for robustness.

06Limitations

  • No stored metrics. Outputs were cleared before commit, so accuracy, loss curves and per-class performance would all have to be regenerated to be citable.
  • The four models were not matched on parameter count or training budget, so the comparison is between four designs rather than a controlled ablation.
  • CNN 4's ceiling is unknown. It was abandoned before it was tuned, so nothing can be said about whether the extra depth would eventually have paid off with more compute.
  • No comparison against a published SVHN baseline, so “97%” has no external reference point attached to it here.

07What I would do next

Re-run CNN 3 with outputs preserved, add a confusion matrix, and compare against a published SVHN benchmark so the figure has something to sit beside. Then test whether augmentation moves it at all — on a dataset this noisy, it plausibly will not, and a null result there would be worth having.
